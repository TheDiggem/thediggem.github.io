Who’s Really Responsible for Protecting Kids? My Take on the ChatGPT Teen Suicide Story

A blunt argument about parental responsibility in the digital age.

By The Diggem · August 30, 2025

There’s been a lot of noise about this tragic story—a 16-year-old who died by suicide after confiding in ChatGPT. His parents are suing OpenAI, claiming the AI gave harmful responses instead of directing him to help (Guardian · People). It’s heartbreaking, no doubt. But here’s my take, plain and simple: this isn’t ChatGPT’s fault. It’s the parents’ responsibility.

Depression Is Real, But Oversight Is Essential #

I’ve battled depression my entire life. At 45, I’m still in therapy and seeing a psychiatrist. I get the weight of those dark thoughts all too well. But here’s the hard truth: if someone’s struggling, they’ll seek out ways to fuel those impulses. If it wasn’t ChatGPT, it could’ve been Google, a library book, a shady forum, or even a toxic friend. The tool doesn’t create the depression—it’s just the outlet they find.

That’s why parents can’t just assume their kids are “fine.” It’s their job to stay vigilant.

Privacy Isn’t a Shield from Responsibility #

This notion that kids have an absolute “right to privacy” is overblown. Sure, to a degree—but if you’re under 18 and under your parents’ roof, that privacy has limits. Parents hold full authority over their child’s phone, computer, social media, and anything tied to their well-being.

Back in my day, I stashed cigarettes and porn in closets or hidden spots. Now, kids bury secrets in apps and devices. The hiding place changed, but the principle hasn’t: kids hide what they know they shouldn’t. Parents who skip the checks are essentially saying, “I’d rather not know.” Ignorance doesn’t prevent the harm—it enables it.

Whose Fault Is It, Really? #

At the end of the day:

  • It’s not ChatGPT’s fault. It didn’t invent the depression or pull the trigger.
  • It’s not “society’s fault.” That’s a lazy excuse.
  • It’s the parents’ responsibility. They should’ve been tracking their child’s mental health, devices, and online interactions. Spotting those chats early might’ve allowed intervention.

This isn’t cruel blame—it’s reality. As the parent of a minor, the buck stops with you.

“A child’s safety trumps their secrecy. Privacy is earned; protection is owed.”

The Parenting Lesson #

If your kid’s under your roof, know what they’re doing online, who they’re talking to, and what’s burdening them. Don’t wait for disaster to check the phone, read the journal, or ask the tough questions. Because once you’ve lost them, no regret can reverse that oversight.
